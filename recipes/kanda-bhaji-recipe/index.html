Kanda Bhaji (Onion Pakoda)

4.8/5 (0 reviews)
Jump to Recipe
Share: WhatsApp Facebook X Pinterest

Kanda Bhaji is the Maharashtrian onion pakoda that belongs to a rainy afternoon — thinly sliced onions mixed with besan, rice flour, green chilli, and ajwain, dropped by hand into hot oil and fried until lacy, crisp, and deep golden at the edges. The centre stays just tender, the edges crunch, and a glass of hot cutting chai on the side completes it. The practical secret is to add almost no water: salt the onions first and let them rest for 10 minutes, and their own moisture is usually enough to bind the batter. A watery batter makes bhaji that soak up oil and go soft within minutes.

Step-by-Step Instructions

8 steps
  1. 1

    Step 1

    Place the thinly sliced onions in a large bowl, add the salt, and mix well with your hands, separating the slices. Rest for 10 minutes so the onions release their moisture.

  2. 2

    Step 2

    Add the besan, rice flour, chopped green chillies, chopped coriander leaves, crushed ajwain, turmeric powder, and red chilli powder to the onions.

  3. 3

    Step 3

    Mix everything together, squeezing gently, until the flours coat the onions evenly and the mixture holds together when pressed. Add the water only if the mixture feels too dry, 1 tbsp at a time — you may not need all of it.

  4. 4

    Step 4

    Heat the oil in a kadhai on medium-high heat, to about 175°C. A small pinch of the mixture dropped in should sizzle and rise to the surface steadily.

  5. 5

    Step 5

    With wet fingers or a spoon, drop small, rough portions of the onion mixture into the hot oil without pressing them into neat balls — loose, uneven shapes fry crisper. Do not overcrowd the kadhai.

  6. 6

    Step 6

    Fry for 3 to 4 minutes, turning once or twice, until the bhaji are deep golden and crisp all over.

  7. 7

    Step 7

    Remove with a slotted spoon and drain on kitchen paper. Repeat with the remaining mixture, letting the oil return to temperature between batches.

  8. 8

    Step 8

    Serve hot, straight away, with green chutney or ketchup and hot tea.

💡

Chef's Pro Tip

For optimal flavor, ensure all fresh spices are freshly roasted or ground before adding. Adjust seasoning gradually according to taste and let the dish rest for 3–5 minutes before serving.

North Indian

Dahi ke Kebab

Dahi ke Kebab is a delicate Awadhi-style party starter with a crisp golden crust and a soft, almost creamy centre made from hung curd and paneer, gently spiced with ginger, green chilli, and garam masala. The contrast is the whole point — crunchy outside, melt-in-the-mouth inside — and it is best eaten hot, straight off the tawa, with green chutney. The one thing this recipe needs is planning: the curd must drain for several hours to become thick enough to shape, and rushing that step is the most common reason the kebabs fall apart. Chilling the shaped kebabs for 20 minutes before frying also helps them hold together neatly.

Gujarati

Methi Thepla

Methi Thepla is a soft, thin Gujarati flatbread made with whole wheat flour, fresh fenugreek leaves, curd, and everyday spices, rolled very thin and cooked with a little oil until golden spots appear. It has a gentle bitterness from the methi, balanced by the spices and the slight tang of curd, and it stays soft for hours — which is exactly why it is a favourite for lunchboxes, train journeys, and picnics. A practical tip: wash and dry the methi leaves well before chopping, and rest the dough for at least 15 minutes. Wet leaves make the dough sticky, and an unrested dough springs back when you try to roll it thin.

45 minutes

North Indian

Vrat Wale Aloo

Vrat Wale Aloo is a simple, comforting potato dish made for fasting days, with boiled potatoes tossed in ghee with cumin, green chilli, ginger, and roasted peanuts, finished with lemon and fresh coriander. It is soft, mildly tangy, and satisfying without being heavy, and it pairs well with kuttu or singhara rotis and curd where those are part of a family's fast. Vrat rules differ by family and region, and this version follows common practice — it uses sendha namak (rock salt) instead of regular salt, and contains no onion, no garlic, and no grains or wheat. Please adapt the ingredients to your own household tradition if yours is different.

30 minutes
